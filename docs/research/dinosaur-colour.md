# What science says about the colour of the 30 Dinodeck animals

Researched 2026-10-03. Purpose: give the repaint a defensible basis and let each card say honestly what is known and what is guessed.

**How sources were checked.** Publisher sites (Nature, Science, Cell, Royal Society, RSC, PNAS) refused automated access, so most papers were read through their Europe PMC record (abstract, and full text where the paper is open access). Each source in the list at the end carries a tag:

- **FT** = full text read.
- **ABS** = abstract read (Europe PMC or Crossref record).
- **META** = only title, authors and journal confirmed. The content claim comes from a secondary source that is named next to it.
- **SEC** = secondary source (press release, review, blog) opened and read.

Claims that rest on META or on nothing are flagged in the text and collected in section 8.

## 1. Summary

- Colour is known from fossil pigment for only **2 of the 30**: *Microraptor* (iridescent, glossy dark plumage) and *Archaeopteryx* (one matte black wing feather, whose owner is disputed). A third, *Diplodocus*, has reported pigment bodies in juvenile skin, but the colour reading was formally challenged in 2026.
- For about **12** there is evidence from a relative: banded chestnut and pale *Sinosauropteryx* (for *Compsognathus*), countershaded brown *Psittacosaurus* (for the ceratopsians), reddish-brown countershaded *Borealopelta* (for *Ankylosaurus*), dark-skinned mosasaur scales (for *Mosasaurus*), black and rufous dromaeosaurs, brown *Beipiaosaurus*, black *Caudipteryx* with a striped tail, pigmented pterosaur crests, pigmented plesiosaur skin.
- For the remaining **15**, including *T. rex*, *Stegosaurus*, *Spinosaurus* and all hadrosaurs, there is no colour evidence at all. Skin texture is known for several (scales on *T. rex*, *Carnotaurus*, hadrosaurs), colour is not.
- What fossils can show is almost only **melanin**: black, grey, brown, rust, and by its absence white or pale. Reds, yellows, greens and blues from other pigments or from skin structure do not survive in a readable way, so "no evidence of bright colour" is not "evidence of no bright colour".
- Pattern is better supported than hue. Dark above and pale below (countershading), tail bands and face masks are seen directly in fossils. Exact hue is an inference from melanosome shape, which is reliable in true feathers and unreliable in skin and simple filaments.
- The idea that big dinosaurs were grey like elephants is an analogy with mammals, not a finding. The one large dinosaur with pigment evidence (*Borealopelta*, about 1 300 kg) was reddish-brown and camouflaged.
- Dinosaurs very probably saw colour at least as well as birds do, so colour signals on crests, frills, plates and sails are reasonable. That they were brightly coloured is still a guess in every case.
- Pure guess in this file: every hue on a display structure, every green or blue, and the whole palette for the 15 "inference only" animals.

## 3. Relatives with known colour

**Sinosauropteryx** (compsognathid, Early Cretaceous, China). Zhang et al. 2010 found melanosomes inside the filaments and inferred "chestnut to reddish-brown" dark stripes on the tail. Smithwick et al. 2017 mapped the pigment over several specimens: banded tail, a bandit mask, and countershading matching the pattern predicted for open habitats. Extension to *Compsognathus*: same family and size, so the pattern is the best available model. The hue is weaker than usually stated, because Li et al. 2014 found that melanosome shape does not track colour in simple filaments.

**Anchiornis** (early paravian, Jurassic). Li et al. 2010: grey and dark body, rufous crown, rufous speckles on the face, white limb feathers with black tips. The first whole-animal reconstruction. Extension: shows that bold banding, pale wing bars and a coloured crown were available to small feathered dinosaurs near *Archaeopteryx*, *Velociraptor* and *Deinonychus*. It does not tell us that those animals had the same pattern.

**Caihong** (early paravian, Jurassic). Hu et al. 2018: platelet-shaped bodies like those behind hummingbird iridescence on the head, chest and tail base, rest of plumage predicted black. The paper itself says this holds "based on the assumption that these structures are melanosomes". Extension: iridescence evolved more than once near the origin of birds.

**Sinornithosaurus** (dromaeosaur). Zhang et al. 2010, as summarised by Roy et al. 2020: rod and sphere melanosomes in different samples, read as black and rufous. Extension: reasonable basis for mixed dark and rusty tones on *Velociraptor* and *Deinonychus*.

**Psittacosaurus** (early ceratopsian). Vinther et al. 2016: one exceptionally preserved specimen, pigment preserved over the whole body. Countershaded with a light belly and tail underside, darker chest, dark face including the cheek horns, pattern on the limbs, heavy pigment around the cloaca. Ovoid melanosome impressions "would suggest a brown color". The gradual shading fits a forest with a closed canopy. Extension: fair for *Protoceratops* (pattern logic, not the forest habitat), weak for *Triceratops*.

**Borealopelta** (nodosaur, about 1 300 kg). Brown et al. 2017: melanin detected chemically in the organic film over the armour, countershading across the body, large shoulder spines lighter than the rest "consistent with display". Roy et al. 2020 note that no melanosome bodies were found and that the reddish-brown reading comes from benzothiazole, a pheomelanin breakdown marker. Extension to *Ankylosaurus*: same larger group, similar build, so reddish-brown with countershading is the most defensible palette in the deck after the two feathered animals.

**Tupandactylus** (tapejarid pterosaur). Cincotta et al. 2022: melanosomes differ in shape between crest skin, simple fibres and branched feathers, a feature otherwise known only from theropods. The authors conclude colour signalling is old. No hue is given. Extension to *Pteranodon*: crests were pigmented and could be patterned. Nothing more.

**Mosasaur, ichthyosaur, leatherback turtle.** Lindgren et al. 2014: eumelanin chemically identified with melanosomes in the skin of all three. The abstract's only statement about pattern is that "at least some ichthyosaurs were uniformly dark-coloured". A later ichthyosaur study (Lindgren et al. 2018, *Stenopterygius*) found pigment cells on the flank and not the belly, which suggests countershading. Extension to *Mosasaurus*: dark upper body is well supported. A pale belly is a guess from living whales and sharks.

**Platecarpus** (mosasaur). Lindgren et al. 2010: the "putative skin color markings" in the abstract turn out in the full text to be reddish stains inside the rib cage, read as remains of internal organs. Not evidence of skin colour.

**Plesiosaur from Holzmaden.** Marx et al. 2025: melanosomes and skin cell layers in smooth tail skin, scales on the flipper. Extension to *Plesiosaurus*: pigmented skin, texture guide for the illustrator. No hue.

**Hadrosaur mummies.** Sereno et al. 2025: the *Edmontosaurus* mummies are a clay film under 1 mm thick formed "before the loss of all soft tissues and organic compounds", so they cannot hold pigment. Manning et al. 2009 found organic compounds in the "Dakota" specimen's skin, and its abstract does not mention pigment. Barbi et al. 2019 found carbon-rich layers in another hadrosaur's skin, again no colour reading. Extension: scale texture, back crest, tail spikes and hooves for *Parasaurolophus* and *Maiasaura*. No colour.

**Sauropod skin.** Gallagher et al. 2021 describe varied small scales in *Diplodocus* sp. Gallagher et al. 2025 report melanosome-like bodies in the same quarry's juvenile skin and suggest brown, possibly speckled. Rossi & McNamara 2026 object that sample provenance is unclear, only 16 to 32 bodies were measured per sample where 50 is the norm, shapes were measured on partly buried bodies, and the feather-based method "is fundamentally incorrect" for skin. Their conclusion: the authors cannot claim two melanosome types or a colour pattern. Pittman et al. 2022 review sauropod skin texture.

**Carnotaurus.** Hendrickx & Bell 2021: skin from shoulder, flank and tail, entirely scaly, large conical scales placed at random. No colour. (Content from a search summary of the abstract. The paper itself was not opened.)

**Eggs.** Wiemann et al. 2017: oviraptorid eggshell contains biliverdin and protoporphyrin, reconstructed as blue-green. Wiemann et al. 2018: pigment in the eggs of all eumaniraptorans sampled, spotted and speckled, none in ornithischian or sauropod eggs. Useful if a card shows a nest.

## 4. Inference rules

**4.1 Countershading reveals the light environment.** Dark back and pale belly cancels the animal's own shadow. In 114 living ruminant species, stronger countershading and a sharper boundary go with open, sunlit habitats (Allen et al. 2012). Applied to fossils: *Psittacosaurus* shaded gradually and low, so forest (Vinther et al. 2016). *Sinosauropteryx* had a sharp boundary high on the flank, so open ground (Smithwick et al. 2017). *Status:* the rule in living animals is well supported. The fossil application is accepted but rests on single specimens. Witton 2023 notes that the function of countershading itself is still argued over (he cites Ruxton et al. 2004 and Rowland 2009, not opened here).

**4.2 Countershading reveals predation pressure.** Living large land herbivores with no predators as adults are not countershaded. *Borealopelta* at 1 300 kg was, which the authors read as strong predation by large theropods that hunted by sight (Brown et al. 2017). Allen et al. 2012 also found countershading stronger in smaller ruminants. *Status:* one fossil. Plausible and widely repeated, not tested further. Consequence for the deck: camouflage patterns are defensible even on big plant-eaters.

**4.3 Large animals are harder to hide, and large land predators are coloured for concealment.** Background matching and disruptive patterns work less well as body size grows (review: Yu et al. 2024). "The vast majority of mammals are shades of grey or brown" and concealment is the main driver (Caro 2013). Witton 2023 argues that every large living land predator is coloured for concealment, so giant theropods probably were too: countershaded in the open, striped or blotched in vegetation, patterns possibly fading with age. *Status:* a reasoned argument from living animals by a palaeoartist and palaeontologist, not a fossil finding. Witton himself stresses it cannot predict a colour scheme, only make some schemes more likely.

**4.4 "Big dinosaurs were drab like elephants."** *Status:* contested, and the weakest rule here. For it: 4.3, and the scarcity of dietary carotenoid pigments on land (Witton 2023, citing Blount 2004 and Biard et al. 2005, not opened here). Against it: most mammals have poor colour vision and descend from nocturnal ancestors, while dinosaurs sit between birds and turtles, which see colour very well (4.6). In roughly 6 000 passerine bird species, larger species are more ornamented, not less (Dale et al. 2015). *Borealopelta* was reddish-brown, not grey. Gallagher et al. 2025 explicitly frame their result as a challenge to "drab, grey" sauropods, though that result is itself disputed. Reasonable position for the deck: earth tones on the body, no obligation to grey.

**4.5 Display structures probably carried signals.** Crests, frills, plates and sails are widely read as social or sexual signals (Knell et al. 2013, who also note such readings are "often controversial"). Hone et al. 2012 argue for mutual sexual selection, Padian & Horner 2011 for species recognition, Knell & Sampson 2011 reply (all three META only). The *Protoceratops* frill grows faster than the body, as signal structures do (Hone et al. 2016, META). Fossil pigment support is thin but real: paler shoulder spines on *Borealopelta*, dark face bosses on *Psittacosaurus*, differently pigmented crest regions in *Tupandactylus*. *Status:* that these structures were signals is the majority view, with the mechanism disputed. That they were brightly coloured is inference. No fossil shows a red, yellow or blue crest.

**4.6 Dinosaurs saw colour.** The gene that makes red retinal oil droplets arose before turtles and archosaurs split, and the authors conclude that red oil droplets "contributed to colour vision in dinosaurs and pterosaurs" (Twyman et al. 2016). Crocodilians later lost part of their colour vision in a "nocturnal bottleneck", so they are a poor model here (Emerling 2017). Koschowitz et al. 2014 argue from the same bracket that dinosaurs had four-colour vision including ultraviolet (META only, no abstract available). *Status:* near consensus, by inference from living relatives. The same Cambridge group calls red skin pigment in dinosaurs "more speculative" (press release).

**4.7 Age and sex.** Bright juvenile patterns that fade in adult reptiles are common (Witton 2023, citing Olsson et al. 2013, not opened here). *Ornithomimus* grew its wing-like arm feathers only as an adult, which suggests a display role (Zelenitsky et al. 2012). Sexual dimorphism in dinosaurs is hard to prove: Saitta 2015 opens with "conclusive evidence for sexual dimorphism in non-avian dinosaurs has been elusive" and then argues for two plate shapes in *Stegosaurus* (*Hesperosaurus*) *mjosi*, a claim that has been disputed. *Pteranodon* crest size differing by sex is a long-standing interpretation (Bennett 1992, META only). In living passerines male and female colour are strongly correlated (Dale et al. 2015). *Status:* contested throughout. For the deck: one adult palette per animal is defensible, and a card may say that males, females and young might have looked different.

**4.8 Pigment budget.** What a large land animal can most easily make is melanin: black, grey, brown, rust, plus white where it is absent. Greens and blues in living reptiles and birds are structural or mixed, and carotenoid reds and yellows come from food (Witton 2023; Roy et al. 2020). *Status:* consensus about living animals. Whether any given dinosaur used the non-melanin options is unknown.

## 5. Limits of the method

- **Only melanin survives readably.** Reviews agree that melanin and melanosomes are what is found. For carotenoids and other pigments, Roy et al. 2020 state that their preservation potential is uncertain or their traces cannot be tied to the animal rather than the environment. The exception is porphyrin pigment in theropod eggshell (Vinther 2020; Wiemann et al. 2018).
- **Structural colour is mostly lost.** Heating and pressure experiments on modern feathers destroyed the structures behind non-iridescent blues while melanosomes survived, and also changed melanosome shape and position (McNamara et al. 2013). Iridescence is the exception, because it is inferred from the melanosomes themselves, as in *Microraptor*.
- **Melanosomes or bacteria?** Moyer et al. 2014 argued that the microbodies overlap with microbes in size and shape and that surface layers look more like biofilm. Lindgren et al. 2015 warn that shape alone is ambiguous and ask for combined shape and chemistry. Colleary et al. 2015 answer with chemical signatures of altered melanin across many fossils and state that this "refutes the hypothesis that such fossil microbodies could be bacteria". Current position: where chemistry confirms melanin (mosasaur, *Borealopelta*, ichthyosaur) the case is strong. Where only shape is used, it is weaker.
- **Shape maps to colour only in some tissues.** Li et al. 2014 sampled 181 living species: melanosome shape and colour are linked in mammals and in maniraptoran dinosaurs including birds. In lizard, turtle and crocodile skin, and in simple dinosaur filaments and pterosaur fibres, shape shows little variety and is "uncorrelated with colour". This undercuts hue claims for *Sinosauropteryx*, *Psittacosaurus* skin and *Diplodocus* skin, and is the core of Rossi & McNamara's 2026 objection. Cincotta et al. 2022 partly reopen the question for pterosaurs.
- **Internal melanosomes contaminate.** Organs carry melanosomes too, which fossilise well and can smear across a carcass (McNamara et al. 2018; Rossi et al. 2019).
- **Tiny samples.** *Archaeopteryx* colour rests on one feather. *Psittacosaurus* and *Borealopelta* on one specimen each. The mosasaur on a patch of scales. Individual, age, sex and season are all unsampled.
- **Probabilities are model outputs.** "95 % black" means the fossil melanosomes fall with black feathers in a statistical comparison with living birds. Adding more bird data changed the *Archaeopteryx* answer from "black" to "matte black, 90 %", and the three-dimensionally preserved subset alone came out iridescent at 79 % (Carney et al. 2020).
- **Methods disagree.** Trace-metal mapping gave pale *Archaeopteryx* feathers with dark tips (Manning et al. 2013). Melanosome analysis gave an all-black feather, and its authors call the other result incorrect (Carney et al. 2020).

## 7. Suggested wording for the cards

**Known**

- SV: Forskare har hittat spår av färg i fossilen, så de här färgerna vet vi faktiskt en hel del om.
- EN: Scientists have found traces of colour in the fossils, so we really do know quite a lot about these colours.

**Informed by relatives**

- SV: Ingen har hittat färgen hos just det här djuret, men vi vet hur nära släktingar såg ut och har utgått från dem.
- EN: Nobody has found the colour of this animal itself, but we know what close relatives looked like and have used them as a guide.

**Plausible guess**

- SV: Ingen vet vilken färg det här djuret hade. Färgerna är en gissning som bygger på djur som lever i dag.
- EN: Nobody knows what colour this animal was. The colours are a guess based on animals that are alive today.

Optional second line for *Archaeopteryx* and *Diplodocus*, where the tag alone hides a dispute:

- SV: Här är forskarna inte överens än.
- EN: Scientists do not agree about this one yet.

## 8. Open questions and things I could not verify

- **Lindgren et al. 2014 full text not read.** Abstract and three press pieces only. That the mosasaur is *Tylosaurus nepaeolicus* (specimen SMU 76532) comes from a search-result summary of the Nature page, not from a page I opened. That the sample was too small to show countershading comes from NBC News, not the paper.
- **Li et al. 2012 full text not read.** The abstract says "predominantly iridescent". "Black" is the standard description in later literature (Hu et al. 2018 plot *Microraptor* as black) and matches how iridescence is produced, but the word is not in the abstract.
- **Gallagher et al. 2025 full text not read** (Royal Society page returned 403). Used: Crossref abstract, phys.org summary, and the full text of the 2026 comment. Whether the authors have replied to the comment is unknown.
- **Triceratops skin ("Lane", Houston Museum of Natural Science).** No formal description found. Only a conference abstract is reported (Larson et al. 2007), not opened. Treat as unpublished.
- **Hendrickx & Bell 2021** (*Carnotaurus*): metadata confirmed, content from a search summary. University of New England press page returned 403.
- **Manning et al. 2013** (*Archaeopteryx* patterning): metadata confirmed, content from ScienceDaily. The claim in Wikipedia-level sources that the *Edmontosaurus* "Dakota" mummy preserves melanin and could yield a monochrome pattern (attributed to Manning and colleagues 2009 and 2015) was **not** confirmed: the 2009 abstract does not mention pigment and the 2015 source was not found.
- **Metadata only, content not read:** Hone et al. 2012, Hone et al. 2016, Padian & Horner 2011, Knell & Sampson 2011, Bennett 1992, Koschowitz et al. 2014, Ji et al. 1998, Czerkas 1992, Christiansen & Tschopp 2010. Czerkas 1992 could not even be confirmed in Crossref (DOI lookup failed). Its existence rests on the Gallagher et al. 2021 abstract ("possible keratinous diplodocid dorsal spines") and a search result.
- **Cited only through Witton 2023:** Caro 2005, Stankowich & Blumstein 2005, Ruxton et al. 2004, Rowland 2009, Olsson et al. 2013, Pembury Smith & Ruxton 2020, Blount 2004, Biard et al. 2005.
- **Wiemann et al. 2018 and *Deinonychus*.** The abstract says pigment was found "in all eumaniraptorans" sampled. That *Deinonychus* was among them comes from press coverage seen in search results only.
- **Unsourced, widely assumed:** most mammals have two-colour vision and nocturnal ancestry (used in 4.4); *Velociraptor* and *Protoceratops* lived in arid open habitats; *Compsognathus* itself preserves no informative integument; Saitta 2015 "has been disputed" (I did not locate the rebuttal).
- **No source found at all** for colour in: *Spinosaurus*, *Allosaurus*, *Giganotosaurus*, *Dilophosaurus*, *Argentinosaurus*, *Brachiosaurus*, *Iguanodon*, *Pachycephalosaurus*, *Plateosaurus*. Absence was checked by search and against the taxon table in Roy et al. 2020, which lists none of them. That review is from 2019 to 2020, so a newer paper could have been missed.
- **Open question for the product:** whether to show juveniles differently from adults. The evidence that exists for sauropods is from juveniles.
